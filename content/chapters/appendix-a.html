Appendix A. CHSH and the Explicit Measurement-Dependent Local Hidden-Variable Model

This appendix contains the technical development underlying the discussion in Section 4. It is intentionally separated from the main narrative so that the conceptual argument can proceed without requiring the reader to follow the full CHSH algebra. The appendix derives the CHSH bound for local hidden-variable models, identifies the precise role of measurement independence, and presents the explicit measurement-dependent local hidden-variable model used in the main text.

The appendix supplies the mathematical support for the conclusions summarized in Section 4. The explicit model is a demonstration of mathematical possibility, not a complete physical account of how the relevant correlations arise. The Block Universe interpretation is distinguished below from the additional measurement-dependent hidden-variable proposal.

A.1 CHSH and Its Assumptions

The CHSH argument has two distinct ingredients. Bell-local response factorization, for a sufficiently specified hidden variable λ, takes the form

P(A,B|x,y,λ) = P(A|x,λ)P(B|y,λ).

Measurement independence is the separate statistical assumption

P(λ|x,y) = P(λ).

The familiar local hidden-variable CHSH bound follows when the relevant assumptions, including both factorization and measurement independence, hold. Neither assumption should be defined as the other. The deterministic derivation below identifies exactly where the common hidden-variable distribution enters.

A.1.1 The CHSH experiment

The CHSH formulation makes the logical structure of Bell's theorem especially transparent. Instead of considering a single measurement setting at each location, Alice has a choice between two settings, a and a′, while Bob has a choice between two settings, b and b′. Each measurement produces an outcome of either +1 or −1.

For any pair of settings, define the correlation

E(a,b) = ⟨AB⟩.

The CHSH combination is then

S = E(a,b) + E(a,b′) + E(a′,b) − E(a′,b′).

The remarkable feature of CHSH is that a local hidden-variable model satisfying measurement independence places a strict upper bound on this quantity.

A.1.2 The local deterministic bound

Begin with the deterministic special case:

A = A(a,λ),    B = B(b,λ),

where every outcome is either +1 or −1.

For a particular value of λ, define

S(λ) = A(a,λ)B(b,λ) + A(a,λ)B(b′,λ) + A(a′,λ)B(b,λ) − A(a′,λ)B(b′,λ).

Factor this expression:

S(λ) = A(a,λ)[B(b,λ)+B(b′,λ)] + A(a′,λ)[B(b,λ)-B(b′,λ)].

Because B(b,λ) and B(b′,λ) can each only be +1 or −1, one of the two bracketed terms must be zero and the other must be either +2 or −2. Therefore

|S(λ)| = 2.

This is an important point: the local algebra itself does not yet use measurement independence. The bound arises for each individual hidden-variable state.

A.1.3 Where measurement independence enters

Under measurement independence, the same probability distribution ρ(λ) is used for all four setting combinations:

E(a,b) = ∫ dλ ρ(λ) A(a,λ)B(b,λ),

E(a,b′) = ∫ dλ ρ(λ) A(a,λ)B(b′,λ),

E(a′,b) = ∫ dλ ρ(λ) A(a′,λ)B(b,λ),

E(a′,b′) = ∫ dλ ρ(λ) A(a′,λ)B(b′,λ).

Because the same distribution appears in all four expressions, they can be combined:

S = ∫ dλ ρ(λ) S(λ).

Since |S(λ)| = 2 for every λ, it follows that

|S| ≤ 2.

This is the CHSH inequality.

The exact point at which measurement independence enters is therefore extremely specific. It is not the algebraic identity that gives |S(λ)|=2. It is the assumption that the four experimentally different setting combinations can all be represented as averages over the same underlying distribution ρ(λ).

If instead the distribution depends on the settings, we have

ρ(λ|a,b),   ρ(λ|a,b′),   ρ(λ|a′,b),   ρ(λ|a′,b′),

and the four terms cannot in general be combined into a single integral of S(λ). The step producing |S|≤2 is then unavailable.

Measurement independence → one common ρ(λ) → CHSH bound.

This is the mathematical opening on which the remainder of the proposal rests.

A.1.4 Deterministic notation and stochastic physics

At this stage it is important not to overinterpret the notation A(a,λ) and B(b,λ). The deterministic treatment is mathematically convenient because it makes the CHSH logic transparent. The broader proposal does not require that nature be deterministic at the hidden-variable level.

A more general local stochastic model would instead specify conditional probabilities such as

P(A|a,λ),    P(B|b,λ).

Locality can then be represented by the factorization

P(A,B|a,b,λ) = P(A|a,λ)P(B|b,λ).

Measurement dependence concerns the relationship between λ and the settings, not whether the response functions themselves are deterministic. The proposal therefore remains compatible with genuinely stochastic underlying physics.

A.2 Quantum Predictions and Experimental Violations

A.2.1 The singlet prediction and CHSH violation

For a pair of spin-½ particles prepared in the singlet state, quantum mechanics predicts the correlation

EQM(a,b) = −cos(θab),

where θab is the angle between the measurement directions.

Choose, for example,

a = 0°,   a′ = 90°,   b = 45°,   b′ = −45°.

The corresponding quantum correlations give

|SQM| = 2√2.

Since

2√2 > 2,

the quantum prediction violates the CHSH bound.

The experimental significance is straightforward: measurements reproduce correlations incompatible with the assumptions used to derive the local, measurement-independent CHSH inequality.

Quantum predictions and Bell-test observations violate the CHSH bound obtained under the complete set of relevant assumptions. The violation therefore excludes that class of models; it does not, by itself, isolate measurement independence as the assumption nature must reject. Nor does the violation demonstrate that a usable faster-than-light signal has been sent.

A.3 An Explicit Measurement-Dependent Construction

A.3.1 The constructive question

The CHSH analysis has established the logical opening, but a logical opening is not yet a physical model. To make the proposal more substantial, we need to show that a measurement-dependent local hidden-variable construction can actually reproduce the quantum correlations.

Such constructions are known in the foundations of physics literature. A particularly simple example, associated with Brans and discussed explicitly by Michael Hall, uses a hidden variable with two binary components. The model is useful here not because it constitutes the complete physical theory being proposed, but because it demonstrates that relaxing measurement independence is mathematically sufficient to construct local response functions reproducing the singlet statistics.

A.3.2 The hidden variable and local responses

Let the hidden variable be

λ = (λ1, λ2),

where

λ1, λ2 ∈ {+1,−1}.

There are therefore four possible values of λ.

Define the local measurement responses simply as

A(x,λ) = λ1,     B(y,λ) = λ2.

These response functions are manifestly local in the relevant sense: Alice's result depends on her local hidden-variable component and not on Bob's measurement direction, while Bob's result depends on his local hidden-variable component and not on Alice's measurement direction.

A.3.3 The conditional distribution

The essential ingredient is the distribution assigned to the hidden variable. Instead of assuming a setting-independent distribution, define

ρ(λ|x,y) = [1 − λ1λ2(x·y)] / 4.

Here x and y are the two measurement directions, represented as unit vectors.

This equation contains the entire departure from measurement independence. The local response functions remain simple. All of the angular dependence required to reproduce the quantum correlation is placed in the conditional distribution of the hidden variable.

In this example the response functions contain no dependence on the other party's setting. The angular dependence appears instead in the conditional hidden-variable distribution. That is the specific trade made by the construction.

A.4 Verification of the Model

A.4.1 Normalization and non-negativity

A probability distribution must be non-negative and must sum to one.

Because λ1λ2=±1 and −1≤x·y≤1, the numerator satisfies

0 ≤ 1 − λ1λ2(x·y) ≤ 2.

Thus

ρ(λ|x,y) ≥ 0.

Summing over the four possible values of λ gives

Σλ ρ(λ|x,y) = [4 − (x·y)Σλλ1λ2]/4 = 1,

because the four values contain equal numbers with λ1λ2=+1 and −1.

The construction is therefore a valid conditional probability distribution.

A.4.2 Local marginal probabilities

A crucial property of the model is that Alice's marginal statistics remain independent of Bob's setting, and vice versa.

Since Alice's outcome is A=λ1, sum over λ2:

P(A=+1|x,y) = 1/2,    P(A=−1|x,y) = 1/2.

Likewise,

P(B=+1|x,y) = 1/2,    P(B=−1|x,y) = 1/2.

Thus, neither observer can infer the distant measurement setting merely by examining the local outcome statistics.

This is important conceptually. The model is measurement-dependent, but the dependence does not provide an operational faster-than-light signaling channel.

A.4.3 The singlet correlation

Because the outcomes are

A = λ1,    B = λ2,

their product is

AB = λ1λ2.

The predicted correlation is therefore

E(AB|x,y) = Σλ ρ(λ|x,y) λ1λ2.

Substituting the conditional distribution gives

E(AB|x,y) = Σλ [1 − λ1λ2(x·y)] λ1λ2/4.

The first term sums to zero because the four hidden-variable states contain equal numbers of positive and negative products. The second term gives

E(AB|x,y) = −x·y.

For unit measurement vectors this is exactly

E(AB|x,y) = −cos(θxy),

which is the singlet correlation predicted by quantum mechanics.

A.4.4 The full singlet joint probabilities

The construction is stronger than reproducing only the average correlation. Since A=λ1 and B=λ2, the joint probability of outcomes a,b∈{±1} is

P(A=a,B=b|x,y) = [1 − ab(x·y)]/4.

This is precisely the singlet-state joint probability distribution for spin measurements along directions x and y.

Thus, the simple model does not merely produce the correct value of E(AB). It reproduces the complete two-outcome probability distribution for the singlet measurement.

No-signaling in the constructed statistics. The local marginal probabilities above are uniformly 1/2 and do not depend on the distant setting. Thus, for the modeled singlet experiment, the observable outcome statistics give neither party a channel for inferring the other's setting. This verifies operational no-signaling for this distribution; it does not independently establish a causal mechanism that realizes the setting dependence.

A.5 What the Construction Establishes

The model demonstrates the following exact mathematical statement: local response functions together with an appropriately setting-dependent hidden-variable distribution reproduce the singlet joint probabilities. In particular, Bell-violating correlations need not be represented by response functions in which Alice's outcome explicitly depends on Bob's chosen setting or vice versa.

It is important to use the Bell terminology precisely. The conditional outcome probabilities in this example factorize into local responses, while the hidden-variable distribution violates measurement independence. These are distinct conditions. The model is therefore properly called a measurement-dependent local hidden-variable model, or a model with local response functions and setting-dependent hidden-variable statistics; it is not a model satisfying all assumptions needed for the usual CHSH bound.

There is a further limit to the construction's explanatory force. With unrestricted measurement dependence, a model of the same mathematical form could encode any target pair-outcome distribution Q(a,b|x,y): set λ=(λ1,λ2), take A=λ1 and B=λ2, and choose ρ(λ|x,y)=Q(λ1,λ2|x,y). The target distribution is then reproduced by construction. The singlet example is an exact and useful existence demonstration, but this flexibility does not explain why nature should realize that particular distribution.

Nor does a local-response factorization, by itself, constitute a complete physical account of local causation. The hidden variables, their relation to the measuring devices, and the origins of the setting-dependent statistics would have to be specified by a physical theory. The construction does not show that measurement independence is violated in nature.

The experiments remain unchanged: observed correlations violate the standard Bell inequalities, and quantum mechanics predicts those correlations. The appendix does not dispute either point. It shows mathematically what becomes possible when one assumption is relaxed, without providing a new experimentally established account of the correlations.

A.6 The Block Universe Interpretation

A.6.1 Correlations within a complete history

In the Block Universe interpretation developed in Sections 1–4, the source preparation, hidden variable, measurement settings, outcomes, observer states, and measurement records belong to one complete four-dimensional physical history. Schematically,

H = {spacetime geometry, preparation, λ, x, y, A, B, records, observer states, ...}.

A statistical dependence between λ and the settings need not, merely by being a dependence, establish that a later setting sends a physical signal into the past or that one distant result dynamically changes the other. Statistical dependence is not itself a causal mechanism. Which causal explanation, if any, applies to a particular dependence is a separate physical question.

The Block Universe does not imply that measurement independence fails. It provides an ontological context in which a proposed measurement dependence can be considered as a relation among events without assuming a new instantaneous or backward-propagating physical influence.

A.6.2 Joint probabilities and possible histories

The statistical relationship can be described by a joint distribution ρ(λ,x,y). The measurement-independence assumption is the factorization

ρ(λ,x,y) = ρ(λ)ρ(x,y).

The present proposal permits a joint distribution that does not factorize in this way. Whenever the relevant conditionals are defined, the statistical dependence may equivalently be written P(λ|x,y) ≠ P(λ) or P(x,y|λ) ≠ P(x,y). Neither conditional form alone specifies a direction of causation.

These distributions describe possible experimental outcomes and their statistical relationships. The actual Block Universe contains the particular events and outcomes that occur. A probability assignment over possible histories must not be confused with a claim that all those alternative histories are physically realized or that the Block Universe itself selects one of them.

A.6.3 Local dynamics and global constraints

Local physical laws describe relationships among physical quantities in spacetime neighborhoods. A proposed global constraint or boundary condition could, in principle, place additional restrictions on which complete histories satisfy those laws. This is a conceptual possibility, not a derivation of the singlet distribution from the Block Universe.

Logical consistency is necessary, but not sufficient, for physical realizability. Global consistency alone neither specifies probabilities nor determines which admissible history is actual. A viable physical model would have to supply the additional laws, boundary conditions, or statistical principles that produce the required correlations.

The result is an interpretive distinction: the complete four-dimensional history may be described as satisfying physical relationships throughout spacetime without treating those relationships as instructions executed by an external time. This perspective does not abolish ordinary local dynamics, and it does not establish that a particular nonlocal-looking statistical correlation has a local causal explanation.

A.7 Randomness, Measurement Choices, and Causality

A.7.1 Ontic randomness and observer records

Statistical dependence should not be confused with determinism. An underlying variable can be genuinely random and also statistically correlated with other physical variables. The example uses deterministic response functions for mathematical clarity, but does not require that the entire underlying physical history be generated by deterministic laws.

Probabilities may characterize alternative possible experimental outcomes under specified preparations and settings, while an individual trial has one definite recorded result. Similarly, one may consider probability assignments over possible complete histories without asserting that all are actual. The Block Universe ontology by itself does not determine the origin or probability of one particular actual history.

An observer encounters physical states and records sequentially along a worldline. Earlier observer states do not ordinarily contain usable records of later measurement outcomes. The four-dimensional description does not imply foreknowledge, a reversal of experienced time, or a physical change to an already completed past.

A.7.2 Measurement settings and free will

Measurement independence is a statistical condition on the relationship between λ and the settings chosen by Alice and Bob. It is not itself a philosophical definition of free will. Conversely, statistical dependence between physical settings and hidden variables does not, on its own, show that experimenters lack agency or have been coerced. Section 5 examines those separate questions directly.

A.7.3 Explanations, objections, and terminology

Proposed violations of measurement independence face a substantive explanatory challenge. The required correlation must be accounted for even when settings are chosen through different physical processes, including human decisions, random-number generators, or astronomical signals. Simply calling the correlation a property of a complete history does not yet explain its magnitude, robustness, or physical origin.

Such proposals are sometimes described using terms including measurement-dependent, retrocausal, and superdeterministic. These labels are not interchangeable. Measurement dependence names the statistical failure of P(λ|x,y)=P(λ). Retrocausal proposals posit a particular causal role for later boundary conditions or events; superdeterministic proposals generally allow the relevant setting–hidden-variable dependencies without adopting ordinary measurement independence. No causal mechanism follows from the conditional probability alone.

The description “conspiratorial” expresses a concern about whether the required correlations can be given a credible physical explanation; it does not by itself prove or disprove the model. The appropriate standard is a developed account, not a rhetorical label. The Block Universe interpretation motivates examining global correlations, but it does not remove the need to explain them physically if the proposal is advanced as a theory.

A.8 Requirements for a Complete Physical Theory

The construction proves an exact representational possibility for singlet measurements. To turn that additional measurement-dependent hidden-variable proposal into a complete underlying physical theory, further work would be required:

  • Physical specification. Identify the relevant hidden variables and give a physically grounded account of their statistical relationship with the measurement settings. Shared causal ancestry or global boundary conditions may be investigated, but neither is established here as the explanation.
  • General formulation. Account for quantum predictions beyond this particular singlet construction, including other states, measurements, interference phenomena, and the established statistical framework.
  • Consistency with established physical constraints. Explain how the correlations fit relativistic causal structure and preserve the absence of operational superluminal signaling, rather than merely reproducing no-signaling marginals in an illustrative distribution.

These requirements apply to the development of a complete physical hidden-variable account, not to the manuscript's separate ontological interpretation of wavefunction collapse. As Section 4.8 explains, treating the wavefunction as a non-ontic statistical description can dissolve the traditional demand for a physical collapse process within that interpretation, without requiring a newly derived hidden-variable theory.

The Appendix therefore establishes a limited but precise mathematical conclusion: local response functions with measurement-dependent hidden-variable statistics can reproduce the full singlet joint probabilities. It does not determine that nature uses this mechanism, explain the origin of the quantum probability distribution, or establish which physically possible history is actual.
