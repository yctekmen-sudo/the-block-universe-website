4 The Block Universe and Quantum Mechanics

4.1 Relativity, the Block Universe, and the Measurement Problem

The Block Universe developed in the preceding sections is based on the four-dimensional spacetime description provided by the theory of relativity. Relativity, and the Block Universe interpretation built upon it, are normally regarded as classical in the sense that the spacetime geometry itself is not treated as a quantum object. In particular, general relativity does not require spacetime, or the gravitational field represented by its geometry, to be quantized.

For the purposes of the argument developed in this section, however, that distinction is not central. We are not attempting to construct a deeper theory that unifies general relativity with quantum mechanics, nor are we attempting to determine the ultimate theory of quantum gravity. The fact that our present theories are expected to be incomplete at certain physical regimes does not prevent us from asking what each theory says within the domains in which it successfully applies.

Both relativity and quantum mechanics are, in important respects, understood as parts of a deeper physical description. General relativity encounters regimes in which its classical description of spacetime is expected to become inadequate, while quantum mechanics and quantum field theory do not by themselves provide a complete description of gravitation and spacetime at the most extreme scales. The existence of these limitations is an important motivation for fundamental physics, but it is not the problem we are addressing here.

Instead, the question is considerably more specific. Quantum mechanics appears to contain a peculiar transition between two apparently different kinds of evolution. According to the Schrödinger equation, a quantum state evolves continuously and deterministically according to a well-defined mathematical rule. Yet when a measurement is made, the theory is conventionally described as undergoing a sudden collapse of the wave function to one of the possible measurement outcomes.

This raises the familiar measurement problem: if the quantum state evolves according to the Schrödinger equation, and if the measuring apparatus is itself a physical system governed by quantum mechanics, what physical process causes the apparently discontinuous transition from a superposition of possible outcomes to a single observed outcome?

The problem is therefore not, at least for the purposes of this discussion, whether quantum mechanics is the final theory of nature. Nor is it necessary to solve the problem of quantum gravity. The question is whether the four-dimensional perspective developed in the preceding sections can provide a different way of understanding what is described as wave-function collapse.

In other words, we will temporarily accept the successful mathematical framework of quantum mechanics and ask whether the apparent mystery of measurement is partly a consequence of the way we ordinarily describe events as unfolding in time. The Block Universe gives us a very different starting point: rather than imagining reality as something that is continuously being created from moment to moment, we begin with the complete four-dimensional spacetime structure. The question then becomes whether quantum measurement needs to be understood as a physical event in which the universe literally selects and creates one outcome, or whether the apparent collapse can be understood differently within the complete spacetime description.

Unitary quantum evolution:   iℏ ∂|ψ⟩/∂t = Ĥ|ψ⟩

Measurement:   |ψ⟩ → |outcome⟩

The second transition is the feature we will examine. The aim is not to dismiss quantum mechanics, nor to replace its experimentally successful predictions with an unsupported alternative, but to investigate whether the Block Universe changes the conceptual interpretation of this transition.

4.2 Entanglement and Superposition Are Distinct Concepts

Before continuing with the discussion of the measurement problem, it is important to distinguish two concepts that are often treated as though they were interchangeable: superposition and entanglement. They are related in quantum mechanics, but they are not the same thing.

A system can be in a superposition without being entangled with another system. For example, a single quantum system may be described by

|ψ⟩ = (1/√2)(|R⟩ + |B⟩).

Here the quantum state is a superposition of two possible states. Nothing about this expression requires the existence of a second system.

Entanglement is instead a property of a joint quantum state. For a pure joint quantum state, two systems are entangled when the state cannot be expressed as a product of independent subsystem states. More generally, a mixed state is entangled if it cannot be represented as a probabilistic mixture of such product states. For example, the pure state

|Ψ⟩ = (1/√2)(|R⟩A|B⟩B + |B⟩A|R⟩B)

cannot be decomposed into a state for A multiplied by an independent state for B. The physical description therefore belongs to the pair as a whole rather than being reducible to two independent quantum states.

This should also be distinguished from ordinary classical correlation. Two colored balls can be correlated—for example, one is red and the other blue—without either ball being in a quantum superposition and without the pair being quantum mechanically entangled. In such a case the balls possess individual physical states, and the correlation simply relates those states.

Correlation ≠ Superposition      Entanglement ≠ Superposition

Quantum entanglement can involve superposition, but the defining feature of entanglement is the non-separability of the joint quantum state. This distinction becomes important when interpreting Bell experiments. Bell's theorem is not simply a statement that quantum systems are in superposition. It concerns whether the observed correlations of entangled systems can be reproduced by a suitable class of underlying local hidden-variable descriptions.

4.3 Bell's Theorem and the Assumptions Behind Bell Inequalities

Bell's theorem addresses a precise question: can the correlations predicted by quantum mechanics, and observed experimentally, be reproduced by a theory in which measurement outcomes arise from underlying physical variables while respecting locality and certain statistical assumptions?

Let λ denote the relevant underlying physical state of a pair of systems. Alice chooses a measurement setting a and obtains an outcome A; Bob chooses a measurement setting b and obtains an outcome B. In a conventional local hidden-variable description, the outcomes are determined probabilistically or deterministically by the local setting and the underlying state. In the deterministic special case this may be written schematically as

A = A(a, λ),    B = B(b, λ).

The locality condition relevant to Bell's theorem is more precise than the ordinary statement that faster-than-light communication is impossible. Bell locality requires that, once the relevant underlying physical state λ is specified, the joint probability of the outcomes factorizes into independent local response probabilities:

P(A,B | a,b,λ) = P(A | a,λ) P(B | b,λ).

In this formulation, Alice's local outcome probabilities depend on her setting and λ, while Bob's depend on his setting and λ. The distant measurement setting or outcome does not provide additional information about the local outcome once these variables have been specified.

This condition should be distinguished from operational no-signaling, which requires that the observable probability distribution of Alice's outcomes cannot be changed by Bob's choice of measurement setting, and vice versa. No-signaling is a weaker condition than Bell locality. Quantum mechanics respects operational no-signaling even though its entangled-state correlations cannot be reproduced by Bell-local, measurement-independent hidden-variable models.

This distinction is important for the present argument. Our objective is not merely to preserve the impossibility of faster-than-light communication, but to examine whether a local hidden-variable description can reproduce quantum correlations when measurement independence is relaxed.

Bell inequalities require more than Bell locality alone. A central additional assumption is measurement independence, also called statistical independence:

P(λ | a,b) = P(λ).

This expresses the assumption that the underlying physical state λ is statistically independent of the measurement settings selected by Alice and Bob. It is often described informally in terms of “free choice,” but the mathematical content is statistical rather than metaphysical. The assumption is not a consequence of special relativity.

Bell's original 1964 argument treated some of these assumptions implicitly, while later formulations made the statistical assumptions more explicit. For present purposes, the important logical point is that the Bell inequality follows only for the class of theories satisfying the relevant Bell assumptions. Consequently, an experimental violation establishes that the observed correlations cannot be reproduced by a theory satisfying all of those assumptions simultaneously.

Bell inequality violated ⇒ at least one required assumption fails.

This statement is weaker, and more precise, than the frequently encountered claim that Bell's theorem proves that physical influences must travel faster than light. Bell-inequality violations establish correlations that cannot be reproduced by Bell-local, measurement-independent hidden-variable models. They do not by themselves identify which assumption must be abandoned, nor do they establish that a usable faster-than-light signal has been transmitted.

4.4 The Experimental Result: What the Bell Correlations Actually Tell Us

The experimental result should first be taken at face value. Quantum experiments produce correlations between spatially separated measurement outcomes that violate Bell inequalities. Whatever interpretation is ultimately adopted, the observed correlations are an empirical fact that any adequate physical account must reproduce.

The usual conceptual question is then framed as follows: if Alice and Bob are separated by a spacelike interval, how can their outcomes be correlated more strongly than permitted by a local hidden-variable model?

A common response is to attribute the correlations to a nonlocal feature of the quantum state, sometimes described informally as “spooky action at a distance.” But the experimental result itself is more limited. It tells us that the correlations cannot be explained by Bell-local hidden-variable models satisfying measurement independence. It does not by itself establish that one measurement physically changes the distant system instantaneously.

This distinction motivates the alternative developed here. Rather than beginning by asking what physical influence travels from Alice to Bob, we ask whether the observed correlation could instead be understood as a relationship among events already contained within a complete four-dimensional physical history.

4.5 The Block Universe and Measurement Independence

The Block Universe provides a natural perspective from which to question measurement independence as a fundamental requirement. In the four-dimensional description developed earlier, the complete physical history contains events at different spacetime locations and times. The fact that one event occurs later along a particular worldline does not mean that it must be statistically independent of every earlier physical variable.

Suppose λ describes an underlying physical property of a quantum system and a and b describe measurement settings that occur later in the experimental history. The conventional statistical-independence assumption requires

P(λ | a,b) = P(λ).

The Block Universe proposal considered here instead allows

P(λ | a,b) ≠ P(λ).

It is important to distinguish what the Block Universe permits from what it logically requires. The existence of a complete four-dimensional physical history does not, by itself, imply that measurement independence must be violated. A Block Universe can contain statistically independent variables just as it can contain correlated ones. The proposal developed here is therefore not that measurement dependence follows necessarily from the Block Universe, but that the Block Universe provides an ontological framework in which such dependence can be considered without automatically invoking a physical influence propagating between distant events.

The meaning of this is important. It does not mean that Alice's or Bob's later measurement sends a physical influence backward in time to alter λ. Nor does it require us to postulate a new force or signal. Rather, the proposal is that the underlying physical variables and the measurement settings may possess statistical correlations as part of the complete four-dimensional history.

One possible physical origin of such correlations is shared causal ancestry. The particles, their source, the measurement apparatus, and the physical systems used to select measurement settings are not entirely isolated entities. Their past light cones may overlap, and their physical histories may include common causes and earlier interactions. Such shared causal histories can, in principle, establish statistical correlations that persist into the experiment. From the Block Universe perspective, these correlations would be ordinary relationships among physical events, not evidence of advance knowledge, backward causation, or a conspiracy between particles and experimenters.

This possibility is offered only as an illustration, not as an established explanation of Bell inequality violations. Shared causal ancestry does not, by itself, demonstrate the particular measurement dependence needed to reproduce quantum correlations. Establishing that connection would require a developed physical model, which is beyond the scope of this manuscript. The present argument requires only that measurement dependence remains a physically conceivable alternative to abandoning locality, not that its precise mechanism has been identified.

This is the central conceptual move of the present argument:

Correlation across spacetime ≠ interaction across spacetime.

In an ordinary dynamical description, one naturally asks what event causes another event to occur. In a Block Universe description, relationships between events can instead be considered as properties of the complete spacetime structure. The absence of a dynamical influence from A to B therefore does not imply the absence of a physical correlation between A and B.

However, this ontological possibility should not be confused with a complete physical explanation. Allowing measurement dependence does not explain why the correlations take precisely the form predicted by quantum mechanics. A fully developed physical theory would need to specify the relevant probability distributions and reproduce the experimentally observed correlations, including their no-signaling properties.

The present proposal makes a narrower claim. It questions whether measurement independence must be regarded as a fundamental physical principle and explores the consequences of relaxing it within a globally consistent Block Universe. Once measurement independence is relaxed, the conventional Bell inequality no longer constrains every local hidden-variable description in the same way.

The Block Universe thus provides a possible ontological interpretation of measurement-dependent correlations, without claiming that the Block Universe alone derives those correlations or establishes that measurement independence is actually violated in nature.

4.6 Ontically Random Hidden Variables in the Block Universe

The hidden variables considered here are not intended to restore a deterministic theory beneath quantum mechanics. This distinction is essential. The proposal retains the possibility that quantum randomness is ontic: the underlying physical variable itself can be genuinely random rather than merely unknown because of incomplete information.

Consider a simplified double-slit experiment involving many photons. Introduce an underlying variable λ with a hundred possible values, where each value labels a possible complete experimental history, including the measurement arrangement and the accumulated detection records. Values 1–10 might correspond to histories in which the experimental record indicates the photon went through the left slit; values 11–20 might correspond to histories indicating the right slit; and values 21–100 might correspond to histories in which no which-slit measurement is performed and the accumulated detections of many photons produce an interference pattern. The last category makes no claim about a particular classical trajectory through both slits. It is merely a label for the observed interference outcome.

The particular value of λ is taken to be genuinely random. Before the relevant event is encountered along an observer's worldline, the observer cannot know which value will be realized. The point is not that the value was secretly determined by some deeper deterministic variable.

What is added is a correlation between λ and the measurement history. If the experimental arrangement includes a measurement of which slit the photon traverses, the value of λ encountered by the observer is correlated with that measurement arrangement. When the measurement records the left slit, the observer discovers a value in the corresponding group. The measurement does not cause the photon to decide, at that instant, which history it had followed. Rather, the measurement event and the photon history are components of one self-consistent Block Universe history.

The same conceptual structure can be extended to Bell experiments. Let λ be an ontically random hidden variable associated with an entangled pair, while a and b are Alice's and Bob's measurement settings. The complete history may contain correlations among

λ,   a,   b,   A,   B.

Those correlations do not require a signal to propagate from Alice to Bob, nor from Bob to Alice. They are simply correlations among physical events contained in the same four-dimensional history. Quantum mechanics supplies the experimentally successful probability structure that those correlations must reproduce, while the hidden variable identifies which particular physical history is encountered along an observer's worldline.

In this picture, quantum mechanics need not be replaced by a deeper deterministic theory. The proposal instead retains the quantum probability distribution and adds an ontological interpretation of the individual outcomes. The hidden variable answers the question, “Which particular outcome occurs in this physical history?” Quantum mechanics answers the statistical question, “With what probabilities are the different outcomes realized?”

Quantum mechanics → statistical structure of possible histories

Block Universe + hidden variables → particular realized history

This is therefore distinct from a proposal in which quantum probabilities are merely epistemic and a deeper deterministic theory is assumed to exist. Such a theory might, in principle, resemble the relationship between microscopic dynamics and macroscopic statistical laws, or between detailed physical dynamics and probabilistic weather forecasts. That is a much stronger hypothesis and is not required for the present argument.

4.7 Measurement as a State Transition Along a Worldline

The Block Universe perspective also changes the conceptual status of measurement itself. In the ordinary dynamical picture, a measurement is often represented as a sudden transition of the quantum state:

|ψ⟩ → |outcome⟩.

This notation naturally suggests that the universe was previously in a superposition and that, at the instant of measurement, some physical process caused one possibility to become real while the others ceased to be real. This is the source of much of the mystery associated with wave-function collapse.

In the Block Universe picture developed here, the measurement can instead be understood as a physical correlation between the measured system, the measuring apparatus, and the observer. The observer is itself a physical system with a worldline. Successive states of that observer may be represented schematically as

S1 → S2 → S3.

At S2, the observer has a physical record of a measurement outcome. The observer therefore experiences the measurement as an event occurring at a particular time. But from the four-dimensional perspective, there is no requirement that the entire universe undergo a sudden, globally propagating collapse at S2. The measurement is one part of the complete spacetime structure.

This does not deny that a quantum measurement produces a physical change in the measuring apparatus or observer. It denies only that this local physical change must be accompanied by an instantaneous change propagating through the entire universal quantum state.

The proposal can therefore be summarized as follows:

Quantum state and its evolution → statistical constraints on physical histories

Ontically random hidden variable → identification of the particular outcome in the actual history

Block Universe → complete four-dimensional consistency of the measurement history

Observer's worldline → sequential encounter with the physical states and records constituting the measurement

On this interpretation, wave-function collapse is not a fundamental physical process occurring throughout spacetime. What is conventionally described as collapse is instead a mathematical representation of the change in information available to the observer as a result of the physical state transition along the observer's worldline.

4.8 Dissolving the Measurement Problem

The traditional quantum measurement problem arises from the apparent conflict between the continuous, unitary evolution of the wavefunction and the definite outcomes recorded in physical measurements. This conflict arises when the wavefunction is assumed to provide a complete description of physical reality, so that a mathematical superposition must somehow become a single actual physical outcome.

The Block Universe interpretation developed here rejects that assumption. The wavefunction is understood as a mathematical description of possible measurement outcomes and their statistical relationships, rather than as a physical entity whose superposition must collapse. The actual four-dimensional universe contains one complete physical history, including definite measurement events, interactions, and physical records. These outcomes do not require a separate physical process to bring them into existence.

Quantum mechanics continues to describe the statistical structure of these outcomes using its existing mathematical formalism. The Schrödinger equation, Born rule, quantum interference, and experimentally observed correlations remain unchanged. What changes is the ontological interpretation of the mathematics, not the mathematics itself.

Within this framework, the traditional measurement problem is therefore dissolved rather than solved by introducing a new physical mechanism. There is no need to explain how a physically existing superposition becomes a definite outcome, because the superposition is not assumed to represent multiple simultaneously realized physical outcomes. The measurement itself remains a real physical interaction, while wavefunction collapse represents an update of the mathematical description associated with the observer's acquisition of a definite physical record.

This conclusion does not establish that the Block Universe interpretation is uniquely correct, nor does it rule out alternative interpretations of quantum mechanics. It establishes a narrower but significant point: once the wavefunction is no longer regarded as a complete description of physical reality, the traditional demand for a physical collapse mechanism disappears.

4.9 The Scope and Test of the Proposal

The argument developed so far should be stated modestly. It does not claim that Bell's experiments are mistaken, that quantum mechanics is empirically incorrect, or that a complete alternative theory has already been constructed. The experimental correlations remain exactly what they are: correlations that violate Bell inequalities.

The proposal instead questions the interpretation of those violations. Bell's result shows that the observed correlations cannot be reproduced by hidden-variable theories satisfying all of the assumptions required to derive Bell's inequality. The present proposal retains locality and quantum randomness but rejects measurement independence, allowing the hidden variables to be statistically correlated with the measurement settings as part of the complete Block Universe history.

The Block Universe interpretation:

  • The actual physical universe consists of one complete four-dimensional history containing definite measurement events and outcomes.
  • The wavefunction describes the statistical structure of possible outcomes rather than constituting a complete description of physical reality.
  • Wavefunction collapse is a mathematical update, not an additional physical process that creates a definite outcome.
  • The established quantum mathematical formalism and its experimental predictions remain unchanged.

The additional proposal examined in the Bell analysis:

  • Hidden variables identify the particular outcomes belonging to the actual physical history.
  • These variables may be ontically random.
  • Measurement independence is relaxed, allowing statistical dependence between hidden variables and measurement settings.
  • Local response functions are retained, without introducing a superluminal signaling mechanism.
  • The Appendix A construction demonstrates that this approach can reproduce the quantum singlet correlations.

The second proposal is not a logical consequence of the first. The Block Universe does not require measurement dependence, nor does the interpretation of wavefunction collapse as a mathematical update depend on establishing measurement dependence. Rather, the Block Universe provides an ontological setting in which measurement-dependent correlations can be considered without requiring instantaneous physical interactions between distant measurement events.

The crucial mathematical test is whether this framework can reproduce the precise correlations observed in Bell experiments while maintaining the stated locality condition. The CHSH formulation provides the cleanest way to make that test explicit. The derivation of the CHSH bound, the exact role of measurement independence, and an explicit measurement-dependent local hidden-variable construction are presented in Appendix A. The main text will use those results rather than interrupt the conceptual development with the full algebra.

4.10 Delayed-choice and Quantum Eraser Experiments

In these experiments, quantum systems are prepared so that which-path information is correlated with another degree of freedom or an entangled partner. A subsequent measurement can be arranged so that the resulting data distinguish paths or reveal interference within appropriately selected subsets.

In some arrangements, the relevant measurement occurs after the signal particle has already been detected. This can create the impression that the later measurement somehow changes whether the earlier particle exhibited wave-like or particle-like behavior. However, the experimentally relevant result is a correlation between measurement records. The unconditioned distribution of earlier detection events does not change according to the later measurement choice. Interference is revealed in particular subsets of the data when the corresponding measurement records are compared.

The Block Universe provides a natural interpretation. All the detection events, measurement settings, and correlations are already part of the complete four-dimensional history. Comparing the records reveals relationships among those events; it does not create or modify the events themselves. No physical signal needs to travel backward through time, and no earlier measurement record needs to be rewritten.

The Block Universe framework developed in this section also clarifies the role of wave-function collapse in these experiments. Quantum mechanics uses the wave function to describe the statistical structure of possible measurement outcomes. When an outcome is obtained, the mathematical formalism updates the quantum state accordingly. This update need not be interpreted as a physical process propagating instantaneously through spacetime. In the proposed Block Universe account, the actual physical history already contains the particular measurement settings, interactions, and outcomes that occurred. The mathematical collapse reflects conditioning the quantum description on an observed result, rather than a physical operation that creates the corresponding history. The delayed-choice aspect therefore introduces no additional physical mystery requiring a special collapse mechanism.

The same conceptual framework applies to the Bell experiments discussed earlier. In both cases, the actual measurement settings and outcomes are components of one complete spacetime history. Our proposal allows correlations between hidden variables and measurement settings, rather than assuming measurement independence as a fundamental requirement.

The delayed-choice and quantum eraser experiments do not independently establish that measurement independence is violated. Their observed correlations can be calculated using standard quantum mechanics. Their significance for the present argument is conceptual: they illustrate how apparently paradoxical relationships between temporally separated events can be understood without requiring physical influences to propagate backward in time.

The Block Universe does not change the experimental predictions. It changes the proposed ontology of the events to which those predictions refer.

Delayed-choice and quantum eraser experiments appear mysterious when physical reality is imagined as a sequence of states that must be progressively created, with later measurements somehow determining the status of earlier events. Within the Block Universe, this difficulty is substantially reduced. The complete physical history contains the actual measurement settings and outcomes. Their correlations are properties of that history, while the quantum formalism describes their statistical relationships. There is no need to introduce a physical mechanism by which a later choice alters an earlier event, or by which mathematical wave-function collapse propagates through spacetime. The apparent paradox arises not from the observed correlations themselves, but from the additional demand that those correlations must be produced by a dynamical influence between the events.

4.11 The Core Result of Section 4

We can now state the principal result of the argument developed in this section. The detailed CHSH derivation and the explicit construction are given in Appendix A; here we summarize what that analysis establishes.

Bell's theorem does not establish that quantum correlations are impossible for every local hidden-variable theory. It establishes that they are impossible for the narrower class of local hidden-variable theories that also satisfy measurement independence and the other assumptions required by the Bell inequality.

Once measurement independence is relaxed, an explicit local-response model can reproduce the exact singlet probabilities:

P(A=a,B=b|x,y) = [1 − ab(x·y)]/4.

The model does this through a setting-dependent hidden-variable distribution:

ρ(λ|x,y) = [1 − λ1λ2(x·y)]/4.

The local response functions remain

A=λ1,    B=λ2.

Therefore, the observed Bell correlation need not be represented mathematically as a direct dependence of Alice's outcome on Bob's setting or vice versa.

4.12 What Has Been Shown—and What Has Not

It is useful to separate the conclusions that follow from the mathematics from the larger interpretation being proposed. The technical derivation supporting these conclusions is collected in Appendix A.

Shown by the construction:

·         Measurement-independent local hidden-variable models satisfy the CHSH bound.

·         Quantum mechanics predicts, and experiments observe, violations of that bound.

·         The CHSH derivation uses a common hidden-variable distribution across the different setting combinations.

·         Relaxing measurement independence removes that particular step.

·         An explicit measurement-dependent model with local response functions reproduces the singlet joint probabilities exactly.

·         The model preserves the usual local marginal statistics and therefore does not create an operational signaling channel.

What Would Be Required for a Complete Physical Theory

The construction presented in Appendix A establishes that the quantum singlet correlations can be reproduced using local response functions when measurement independence is relaxed. This demonstrates the mathematical possibility of the proposed approach, but it does not constitute a complete underlying physical theory.

To develop the proposed hidden-variable account into such a theory, the following would be required:

·        A physical specification of the hidden variables and their correlations with measurement settings. The theory would need to identify the relevant physical variables and provide a physically grounded account of the statistical relationships represented by the setting-dependent distribution. The shared causal ancestry discussed in Section 4.5 illustrates one possible direction, but it is not yet a developed physical model.

·        A general mathematical formulation. The theory would need to reproduce the experimentally established predictions of quantum mechanics beyond the particular singlet correlations demonstrated in Appendix A, including other measurement arrangements and physical systems.

·        Consistency with established physical constraints. The theory would need to specify how its physical variables and correlations operate consistently with relativistic causal structure, while preserving the experimentally observed absence of superluminal signaling.

These requirements concern the development of a complete underlying physical theory, not the validity of the Block Universe as an ontological interpretation. The interpretation developed in this manuscript retains the existing quantum mathematical framework and its empirical predictions. It does not require an independently constructed hidden-variable theory to interpret wavefunction collapse as a mathematical update rather than a physical process.

As argued in Section 4.8, the traditional measurement problem is dissolved within this interpretation by rejecting the assumption that the wavefunction is a complete description of physical reality. Constructing a deeper physical theory would be a separate undertaking.

The mathematics also raises a separate philosophical question: if a complete four-dimensional physical history contains the future as well as the past, in what sense are the choices made by observers genuinely free? Section 5 addresses that question.

4.13 The Hidden Variable as Part of a Complete History

The complete history can now be represented schematically, building on the technical construction developed in Appendix A:

H = {spacetime geometry, preparation, λ, settings, outcomes, records, observer states}.

From the perspective of an observer moving along a worldline, these components are encountered sequentially. From the Block Universe perspective, they form one complete four-dimensional physical structure.

The hidden variable therefore does not need to be imagined as a little object carrying a secret message from the source to the measurement devices. It is better understood, at this stage, as part of the specification of the complete physical history.

The key unresolved issue is then no longer simply how the hidden variable travels from one place to another. The deeper question becomes why the complete history has the correlations that it does.
