1 From Relativity to the Block Universe

Central claim of this section: Special relativity establishes that there is no observer-independent global present for spatially separated events. Minkowski spacetime provides a natural four-dimensional description of this structure. The Block Universe is a philosophical interpretation of that four-dimensional structure—not a theorem that special relativity proves by itself.

1.1 Newtonian Absolute Time and the Universal Present

In classical Newtonian mechanics, time is treated as an absolute, universal parameter: a single temporal ordering that, in principle, applies identically to all observers regardless of their location or state of motion. Within this framework, the universe can be pictured as progressing through a sequence of three-dimensional spatial states, each associated with the same universal time.

This picture naturally supports presentism, the philosophical view that only the present moment exists, while the past no longer exists and the future does not yet exist. It is important, however, to distinguish this philosophical interpretation from Newtonian mechanics itself: Newtonian physics supplies an absolute time parameter, but it does not by itself constitute a complete philosophical argument for presentism.

1.2 Einstein's Spacetime: From Separate Space and Time to Minkowski Geometry

Special relativity replaces Newton's absolute time with a spacetime structure in which spatial and temporal coordinates depend on the inertial frame used to describe events. There is no privileged inertial frame from which a universal temporal slicing of the universe can be defined.

Observers in relative motion can disagree about spatial distances and temporal durations. More importantly for the question of now, they can disagree about whether two spatially separated events are simultaneous.

Although the individual quantities Δx and Δt vary between inertial frames, the spacetime interval remains invariant:

Δs2 = -c2Δt2 + Δx2 + Δy2 + Δz2

Here c is the invariant causal speed of relativity. In ordinary units it also relates temporal and spatial coordinates: multiplying a time interval by c gives a quantity with units of length. This permits time to be represented geometrically alongside the spatial coordinates as ct, x, y, z.

The resulting Minkowski spacetime is a four-dimensional geometric structure. An object's history can be represented within it by a worldline: a continuous curve through spacetime. This geometrical description does not by itself require the additional philosophical claim that time literally “does not pass”; rather, it provides a framework in which an object's entire spacetime history can be represented as a single geometric object.

1.3 What Does “Now” Mean?

The word “now” can refer to several different things, and separating them is essential to understanding relativity.

1.          Observed now: the events whose light signals are currently reaching an observer. For distant objects, this is affected by the finite propagation time of light.

2.          Relativistic simultaneity: the distant events that an observer's inertial coordinate system assigns the same time coordinate as the observer's current event. These events form a hypersurface of constant time in that frame.

3.          Ontological existence: the philosophical question of whether events in the past, present, and future exist in the same sense. Special relativity does not, by itself, settle this ontological question.

The crucial point: The relativity of “now” concerns the second meaning. Two observers in relative motion can disagree about which distant events are simultaneous with their common local event. They are not merely receiving different visual information; their inertial coordinate systems assign different simultaneity relations to spatially separated events.

1.4 The Relativity of Simultaneity

Figure 1 is a two-dimensional representation of Minkowski spacetime. A complete representation of Minkowski spacetime is four-dimensional, with three spatial dimensions and one time dimension, and therefore cannot be directly depicted in an ordinary two-dimensional diagram. For clarity, the figure suppresses two of the spatial dimensions and displays only one spatial dimension together with the time dimension. This simplification does not affect the generality of the argument: the relativistic relationships illustrated in the diagram are properties of four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime, and the conclusions drawn from them apply to the full 4D spacetime description.

Consider three inertial observers who pass through the same local event at the origin of a Minkowski diagram:

·             Observer A: at rest relative to the unprimed coordinate system, with v = 0.

·             Observer B: moving at a positive relativistic speed v' relative to A.

·             Observer C: moving at a still greater speed v'', where v'' > v'.

For an inertial frame moving at speed v relative to A, the Lorentz transformation is

t' = γ(t - vx/c2),      x' = γ(x - vt),      γ = 1/√(1-v2/c2)

The key feature is the term vx/c2 in the transformation of time. The time coordinate assigned to a distant event depends on both its time coordinate and its spatial position in the original frame.

For an event simultaneous with the origin according to the moving observer, t' = 0. Therefore:

t' = 0  ⇒  t = vx/c2

Thus, except at x = 0, the events that B considers simultaneous with the origin do not generally satisfy t = 0 in A's frame. This is the mathematical expression of the relativity of simultaneity.

1.5 Event X: How the Same Distant Event Can Be Future, Present, or Past

Now consider a distant event X, separated from the common origin by a spatial distance d > 0. Choose X so that it is spacelike separated from the origin. This qualification is essential: only spacelike-separated events can have their temporal ordering or simultaneity relation changed by a change of inertial frame.

Suppose the velocity parameters are chosen so that X lies on B's hypersurface of simultaneity. Then:

1.          Observer B: Event X satisfies t' = 0. B therefore assigns X the same coordinate time as the origin. In the precise relativistic sense, X is simultaneous with B's present event.

2.          Observer A: The same event X has t > 0. A therefore assigns X a later time than the origin: X lies in A's future.

3.          Observer C: For a sufficiently different relative velocity, the same event can have t'' < 0. C therefore assigns X an earlier time than the origin: X lies in C's past.

Nothing contradictory has happened. The three observers are using different inertial coordinate systems to describe the same two spacetime events. The disagreement arises because simultaneity for spatially separated events is not invariant.

What is—and is not—relative: For spacelike-separated events, observers can disagree about which happens first or whether they are simultaneous. For timelike - or lightlike - separated events, the causal ordering is invariant: all inertial observers agree on which event can causally precede the other.

1.6 Why There Is No Universal “Now”

The Event X example reveals the central conceptual consequence of special relativity. If A, B, and C are all inertial observers and none is physically privileged, there is no frame-independent rule that identifies one particular distant hypersurface as the present moment of the universe.

In other words, the following question has no observer-independent answer within special relativity:

“Which events happening at distant locations are occurring right now?”

A can give one answer. B can give another. C can give another. Their answers are not different because one observer has made a measurement error. They arise from the Lorentz transformation and the geometry of Minkowski spacetime.

This is the precise sense in which the “now” is relative.

Relativity of “now”: There is no single, observer-independent three-dimensional hypersurface of distant events that all inertial observers must identify as the present. Each inertial frame defines its own hypersurfaces of simultaneity.

1.7 From Local Reality to Observer-Independent Existence

Consider a physical event X and an observer D located at X when it occurs. For D, the event is locally real. No inference about distant events, delayed signals, or philosophical interpretation is required to establish this starting point.

Now introduce another observer, B. Suppose initially that B and D coincide at X. Since they encounter the same local event, there is no question that X is physically real for both.

Next, consider B at a small spatial separation from D, in an inertial frame whose simultaneity hypersurface through B's present event also contains X. We ask a simple question: Does X cease to exist merely because B is no longer located at X?

We adopt the principle that it does not. The physical reality of an event is independent of which observer considers it.

The separation between B and D can be increased, and the construction repeated across arbitrarily small increments. In this way, local physical reality is extended across spatially separated events without requiring a privileged observer.

Special relativity now supplies the decisive additional ingredient: the relativity of simultaneity.

By changing B's inertial frame, an event X that was simultaneous with B's present can instead be assigned to B's coordinate past or future, provided the two events are spacelike separated. Nothing about X changes under this transformation. Only the temporal coordinates assigned to it change.

If X is physically real for D, and physical existence is observer-independent, X cannot cease to exist for B merely because B's frame assigns it to the past or future.

The argument therefore rests on a single ontological principle:

An event that is physically real for its local observer is equally real for any other observer, independently of their spacetime locations and reference frames.

1.8 Constructing the Four-Dimensional Block Universe

The preceding reasoning can be extended throughout spacetime.

Begin with an arbitrary locally real event. Its reality is independent of the observer used to describe it. Relativity of simultaneity allows the same event to occupy different temporal positions relative to the present events of other observers. Since those descriptions cannot change its physical existence, reality cannot be confined to any one observer's simultaneity hypersurface.

Repeating the construction across observers and events produces a connected, four-dimensional physical reality.

The Block Universe is therefore not introduced as an independent assumption that past, present, and future must all exist. Rather, it emerges from applying observer-independent physical existence consistently within the spacetime structure of special relativity.

A presentist may introduce an additional preferred foliation to define an objectively privileged present. Such a hypothesis is logically possible, but it is not supplied or required by special relativity. The four-dimensional construction needs no such addition.

The resulting distinction is straightforward: special relativity supplies the relationships among spacetime events; observer-independent physical existence supplies the ontological premise; and the static Block Universe is the resulting interpretation of physical reality.

Here, *static* does not mean that physical quantities are constant over time. It means that the complete four-dimensional history does not require an additional external time in which the universe itself advances from one present to another.

1.9 General Relativity: Curved Spacetime and Dynamical Geometry

Special relativity is sufficient for the argument developed above for the Block Universe. The discussion of general relativity is included here for completeness, to show how the same four-dimensional spacetime framework is extended when gravity is taken into account. General relativity introduces the curvature of spacetime, in contrast to the flat spacetime of special relativity, as the geometric description of gravity. The arguments made so far, however, do not depend on spacetime being flat: they concern the four-dimensional nature of spacetime and the observer-dependence of temporal relations. Accordingly, the essential conclusions extend from flat Minkowski spacetime to curved spacetime as described by general relativity.

General relativity extends the geometric description from flat Minkowski spacetime to curved spacetime, represented mathematically by a four-dimensional manifold (ℒ, gμν). The gravitational field is encoded in the spacetime metric, and the metric is dynamically related to matter and energy by the Einstein field equations:

Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG/c4)Tμν

Unlike the flat geometry of special relativity, the geometry of general relativity need not be static. The Einstein field equations are dynamical equations, and particular solutions can describe evolving gravitational fields, gravitational radiation, expanding or contracting cosmological geometries, and other time-dependent phenomena.

Nevertheless, individual physical histories can still be represented within a four-dimensional spacetime geometry. Worldlines of matter and trajectories of light are curves in that geometry, while the geometry itself is determined by the gravitational field equations together with appropriate initial or boundary conditions.

Thus, general relativity preserves and deepens the geometric lesson of special relativity without requiring the claim that spacetime as a whole is universally static.

1.10 Summary: From Relativity to the Block Universe

1.          Newtonian mechanics contains an absolute time parameter and therefore permits a universal notion of simultaneity.

2.          Special relativity replaces absolute time with frame-dependent temporal coordinates within Minkowski spacetime.

3.          For spacelike-separated events, one observer can assign an event to the future, another to the present, and another to the past. Therefore, special relativity contains no observer-independent global “now.”

4.          The finite speed of information limits when an observer can learn about a distant event; it does not by itself determine when that event exists.

5.          If an event X is physically real for an observer local to X, the later receipt of information about X by another observer does not create X at the moment of reception.

6.          Consequently, the temporal labels past, present, and future can be understood as observer-relative relations to events that belong to the same four-dimensional physical reality.

7.          On this ontological interpretation, past, present, and future all physically exist, and the resulting picture is the Block Universe.

Core conclusion: Special relativity removes the possibility of a single observer-independent global “now.” The finite speed of information does not rescue a distinction between “what exists” and “what has not yet been observed,” because an event can exist independently of when information about it reaches a particular observer. Once that ontological principle is combined with relativity of simultaneity, the past, present, and future can be understood as different observer-relative temporal relations to events within one four-dimensional physical reality. This is the Block Universe picture.
