2 The Block Universe and Direction of the Flow of Time

2.1 From the Existence of Time to the Flow of Time

Section 1 established the Block Universe as a four-dimensional spacetime in which past, present, and future events all physically exist. The entire history of the universe can therefore be regarded as a single four-dimensional entity extending across the temporal as well as spatial dimensions. For the purposes of this discussion, we will take this block to encompass the complete history of the universe, extending back toward the Big Bang and its associated initial singularity. Every event contained within the spacetime—including events that we describe as past, present, or future—statically exists as part of the complete four-dimensional structure.

This raises a different question. If the entire spacetime exists as a whole, and time is simply one of the coordinates of that spacetime, why do we experience time as flowing in a particular direction?

The distinction between time and the flow of time is essential here. Within the Block Universe, the temporal coordinate provides an ordering of events just as spatial coordinates provide an ordering of locations. Nothing in the existence of a temporal coordinate by itself requires spacetime to physically move from one time to another. There is therefore no need to introduce a fundamental substance or process that causes the universe to advance through time.

We may therefore distinguish three related but different concepts:

  • Time as a coordinate: the temporal dimension used to locate events within spacetime.

  • Temporal ordering: the ordering of events along a timelike worldline, such as the sequence of physical states experienced by an observer.

  • The experienced flow of time: the perception by a physical observer that it is progressing from one state to another, with a definite distinction between past, present, and future.

The first two are structural properties of spacetime and physical systems within it. The third is a property of an observer embedded within spacetime. The Block Universe therefore does not need to flow through time in order for an observer within the block to experience a flow of time.

In this sense, the flow of time is not a fundamental property of spacetime itself. It is a description of the experience of physical systems as they occupy successive states along their worldlines.

The Block Universe does not flow through time; observers experience a flow of time within the Block Universe.

2.2 The Observer as a Physical System in the Block

To understand this experienced flow, consider an observer as a physical system embedded within the four-dimensional spacetime of the Block Universe. The observer has a worldline extending through spacetime, and at different points along that worldline its physical state is different.

Let the physical state of the observer at three successive values of its temporal coordinate be represented by:

(T1, S1)     (T2, S2)     (T3, S3),     T1 < T2 < T3.

All three states are part of the Block Universe. Nothing about the block itself requires us to declare that the sequence is physically flowing from S1 to S2 to S3. The reverse ordering can equally be described geometrically by traversing the same worldline in the opposite coordinate direction.

At this stage, therefore, we have established temporal order but not yet the direction of experienced time.

The question becomes: what physical property of the observer distinguishes one direction along its worldline as the direction of experienced time?

2.3 The Information Structure of Successive Brain States

For a human observer, the relevant physical system is the brain. The human brain is itself an entity within the four-dimensional spacetime of the Block Universe. As the brain's worldline extends through spacetime, the physical state of the brain changes from one point to another.

Suppose that the brain is in state S2. That state contains physical information resulting from its interaction with the world at earlier points along its worldline. The brain may contain memories of previous experiences, records of previous events, learned information, physical modifications resulting from earlier interactions, and many other forms of information that are consequences of earlier states.

Consequently, the state S2 contains information about aspects of S1. The later state therefore carries physical records of the earlier state.

S1  →  S2:    S2 contains information about S1.

By contrast, the state S2 does not ordinarily contain corresponding information about the future state S3. The brain at S2 does not ordinarily contain memories or physical records produced by events that have not yet occurred along its worldline.

S2  →  S3:    S2 does not ordinarily contain information about S3.

This creates an information asymmetry between the two directions along the observer's worldline. One direction is associated with states that have already left information in the current state; the other is associated with states that have not yet done so.

This asymmetry provides a physical basis for the observer's distinction between past and future. If S2 is the brain's present state, then S1 is experienced as part of the past because information about it is represented in the present state of the brain. Conversely, S3 is experienced as part of the future because information about it is not yet represented in the brain's present state.

The experienced distinction can therefore be represented schematically as:

Past  ←  S1    |    S2    |    S3  →  Future

with S2 representing the observer's present physical state.

The important point is that this distinction does not require the future to be nonexistent. S3 already exists as part of the Block Universe. What distinguishes it from S1 from the perspective of the brain is not its ontological status, but the information contained in the brain's current physical state.

Past, present, and future are therefore not necessarily three different modes of existence within the Block Universe. They are three different relationships between an observer's present physical state and other regions of spacetime.

2.4 The Direction of Experienced Time

We can now identify what is meant by the direction of the flow of time for an observer.

The brain does not merely occupy a sequence of physical states. Its successive states contain records of events associated with preceding states. The direction in which these records accumulate establishes an asymmetry along the brain's worldline.

At S2, the brain contains information about S1, while it does not ordinarily contain information about S3. The brain therefore experiences S1 as past and S3 as future.

In this sense, the direction of experienced time can be associated with the direction of the observer's worldline along which physical records of earlier states are established in later states.

It is important, however, not to equate this simply with the statement that the brain must monotonically accumulate information. A brain can forget information, lose memories, or undergo local decreases in stored information. The relevant property is more fundamental: the causal-information structure of successive physical states is asymmetric. Later states ordinarily contain records and consequences of earlier states, while earlier states do not contain corresponding records produced by later states.

The direction of experienced time is therefore not defined by the absolute quantity of information stored in the brain. Rather, it is defined by the asymmetric orientation of information and physical records along the observer's worldline.

2.5 The Thermodynamic and Gravitational Origin of the Information Asymmetry

The existence of an information asymmetry in the brain raises a further question. Why should physical records be oriented in this particular temporal direction at all? Why should later physical states contain records of earlier states rather than the reverse?

This is where the thermodynamic history of the universe enters the argument. The universe possesses a profound thermodynamic asymmetry. The early universe was in an extraordinarily low-entropy state, while entropy has generally increased through the subsequent evolution of the universe. This provides an intrinsic physical asymmetry within the Block Universe itself.

However, the low-entropy condition is not the whole physical story. The early universe was extremely homogeneous and isotropic on large scales, but it was not perfectly homogeneous. Small primordial density fluctuations were present. These fluctuations provided the seeds for gravitational structure formation: under the influence of gravity, initially small differences in density could grow, eventually producing galaxies, stars, planets, and the complex physical environments in which chemistry, biology, information processing, and observers could arise.

This point is especially important because gravity changes the way we think about entropy. A highly smooth matter distribution can correspond to very low gravitational entropy, whereas gravitationally clumped structures—most dramatically, black holes—represent states of much greater gravitational entropy. The early universe therefore combined an exceptionally smooth large-scale matter distribution with small fluctuations capable of growing into structure. The subsequent formation of gravitational structure is part of the physical history through which the universe developed from its unusually low-entropy initial condition into the complex, high-entropy states found later in the Block.

The primordial density fluctuations should not, however, be identified as the fundamental source of the direction of experienced time. Rather, they are an important physical ingredient in the mechanism by which the thermodynamic asymmetry of the early universe becomes expressed through the formation of structure and irreversible processes. The low-entropy boundary condition supplies the fundamental asymmetry, while the initial fluctuations allow gravitational evolution to generate the structured physical systems in which that asymmetry can be manifested.

The important point is that the thermodynamic asymmetry should not simply be identified with the direction of experienced time. Saying that entropy increases toward one end of the block does not, by itself, explain why that direction should be experienced by an observer as the future rather than the past.

Instead, the thermodynamic and gravitational asymmetries provide the physical conditions under which temporally asymmetric processes and records can arise. As structure forms and physical systems interact, information about preceding states becomes encoded in subsequent states through processes such as measurement, interaction, memory formation, and the formation of physical records. These processes are thermodynamically irreversible at the macroscopic level and therefore contribute to the asymmetric distribution of physical information across successive states.

Thus, the relevant chain of explanation is not simply:

Low entropy  →  flow of time.

Nor is it necessary to treat primordial inhomogeneity as an independent arrow of time. A more complete physical chain is:

low-entropy initial condition  →  primordial density fluctuations  →  gravitational structure formation  →  thermodynamically irreversible processes  →  asymmetric formation of physical records  →  information asymmetry in observers  →  experienced direction of time.

In this formulation, entropy does not define the direction of experienced time. Rather, the low-entropy initial condition establishes the fundamental thermodynamic asymmetry of the universe, while primordial fluctuations provide the seeds through which gravitational structure and increasingly complex physical systems develop. The resulting thermodynamically asymmetric processes produce information-bearing records in a preferred temporal orientation. Human observers, as physical information-processing systems embedded within this history, inherit that information asymmetry, which in turn provides the physical basis for their experience of a directed flow of time.

2.6 Why Local Entropy Decreases Do Not Reverse the Flow of Time

This distinction also resolves an apparent difficulty with identifying the direction of time directly with entropy.

Entropy is not required to increase monotonically in every local region of spacetime. Local entropy can decrease, provided that this decrease is compensated by a sufficient increase elsewhere so that the total entropy of the appropriate closed system increases. Consequently, there can be local fluctuations and processes in which entropy decreases even while the overall thermodynamic arrow remains unchanged.

If the direction of experienced time were simply defined as the direction of increasing local entropy, an observer might appear to experience a reversal of time whenever it entered a region in which local entropy happened to decrease. This is not what we observe.

Our experience of time therefore cannot be identified directly with the local entropy gradient.

Instead, the relevant connection is between the thermodynamic asymmetry of the physical environment and the asymmetric formation of records within physical systems. A local fluctuation in entropy does not, by itself, reverse the causal and informational structure of an observer's physical states. The observer continues to contain records of what we call the past and not of what we call the future.

Thus, the direction of experienced time is a property of the observer's information-bearing physical state, while the thermodynamic arrow provides the broader physical asymmetry from which this information structure emerges.

2.7 Past, Present, and Future as Relations Within the Block

We can now return to the central premise of the Block Universe.

At the level of the complete four-dimensional spacetime, S1, S2, and S3 all exist. Nothing has been created when the brain reaches S2, and nothing ceases to exist when the brain passes beyond it. The entire sequence is already part of the four-dimensional structure.

Nevertheless, the brain at S2 has a particular informational relationship to the rest of spacetime. It contains records of states associated with S1, while it does not yet contain corresponding records of S3. It is this asymmetric informational relationship that gives rise to the observer's distinction between past and future.

The familiar human experience of temporal passage can therefore be understood without requiring time itself to flow through the Block Universe.

The past is not a region of spacetime that has ceased to exist. The future is not a region of spacetime that has yet to come into existence. Both are already present in the block. What changes from one point along the observer's worldline to another is the physical state of the observer and the information encoded within that state.

Conclusion: The Block Universe is static with respect to its complete four-dimensional existence, but it is not temporally symmetric in its physical contents. The low-entropy condition of the early universe establishes a thermodynamic asymmetry within the block. That asymmetry gives rise to a corresponding asymmetry in the formation of physical records. Human brains, as physical information-processing systems embedded within spacetime, inherit this asymmetry: later brain states contain records of earlier states, while earlier states do not contain records of later states. The brain consequently experiences the direction of this information asymmetry as the flow of time, distinguishing past, present, and future. Thus, the flow of time is not a fundamental motion of the Block Universe itself, but an emergent feature of observers physically embedded within its asymmetric structure.
